# The Most Reluctant Convert

*A reflection on C. S. Lewis, Paul's conversion, and what it means to be dragged to a Person.*

---

In Acts 26:14, the risen Jesus tells the man who had been hunting down Christians to execute them:

> "It is hard for you to kick against the goads."

Goads were sharp sticks used to prod and direct an animal. When an ox pushed back against the goad, it only drove the point deeper. Jesus was telling Saul of Tarsus: *you have been fighting me, and you are losing.* The harder you resist, the more you hurt yourself. Stop fighting. Submit.

Saul had not been an indifferent bystander to Christianity. He was its most energetic enemy — holding coats at Stephen's stoning, dragging men and women from their homes, voting for their deaths. His resistance was not passive. It was ferocious. And yet Jesus describes even that ferocity as the kicking of an animal against the prod that is guiding it. The persecution itself was part of the goading.

---

## "The Most Dejected and Reluctant Convert in All England"

Fourteen centuries later, an Oxford don named Clive Staples Lewis would describe his own conversion in terms that illuminate Paul's experience precisely.

In *Surprised by Joy* (1955), Lewis described a different kind of persecution — an intellectual one. He had spent years building careful philosophical arguments against Christianity, only to find them collapsing one by one. He wrote of the relentless approach of the God he was trying not to believe in:

> "You must picture me alone in that room in Magdalen, night after night, feeling, whenever my mind lifted even for a second from my work, the steady, unrelenting approach of Him whom I so earnestly desired not to meet."

He described his final surrender in Trinity Term of 1929:

> "I gave in, and admitted that God was God, and knelt and prayed: perhaps, that night, the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England."

Earlier in the same chapter, Lewis describes the experience of the Prodigal Son — and the phrase he reaches for is striking in light of Acts 26:

> "...brought in kicking, struggling, resentful, and darting his eyes in every direction for a chance of escape."

Lewis was not writing about himself in that sentence. But he might as well have been.

---

## Not a System, but a Person

What made Lewis's conversion — and Paul's — so disorienting was not primarily an intellectual shift. Both men had to revise what they were coming *to*.

Paul expected, at most, to be accepting a set of propositions about a dead teacher's resurrection. Instead he met, on the Damascus Road, a living Person who knew his name and called him by it: *"Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?"* (Acts 9:4). Not *"why are you persecuting my followers?"* — but *me*. The risen Jesus took the persecution personally. He and his people were one.

Lewis described the same surprise from the other direction. He had spent years examining Christianity as a philosophical system — evaluating its arguments, testing its logic. What he was not prepared for was the discovery that the system had a Face. He wrote in *A Grief Observed*:

> "I need Christ, not something that resembles Him."

That is the cry of someone who has discovered the difference between a doctrine and a Person — and found that the Person is the whole point.

---

## The Goad and the Grace

The remarkable thing about both conversions is that resistance was part of the story, not an obstacle to it.

Paul's ferocity against the church was not a detour from his calling — it was, mysteriously, the road toward it. His intimate knowledge of Jewish law, his Roman citizenship, his trained rhetorical mind, his fearlessness in the face of crowds and kings — all of it forged in opposition to Christ — became the precise equipment Christ needed for the mission Paul would carry out. God did not start over with a more cooperative raw material. He redeemed the resistance itself.

Lewis's years of careful anti-Christian argument had the same paradoxical effect. His *Mere Christianity*, *The Problem of Pain*, *Miracles* — the books that have brought millions to faith — were written by a man who had spent decades constructing the opposing case and had watched it fall apart from the inside. His credibility as a defender of Christianity came directly from the depth and seriousness of his former opposition to it.

This is what it means for God to be the one with the goad rather than the convert having the initiative. The prodding is purposeful. The resistance is not wasted. The reluctance is part of the formation.

> *"Faithfully serve the Lord Jesus and keep trusting in him to work even when you can't see what he's doing."*
> — from the Acts 25 study notes

## A Note on the Famous Paraphrase

The quote often attributed to Lewis — *"I came to Christianity kicking and screaming, but I was coming to a Person"* — does not appear verbatim in his writings. It is a paraphrase that accurately captures the spirit of two separate passages in *Surprised by Joy*, Ch. XIV. The actual quotes are those given above. If you use Lewis in conversation or teaching, the real words are worth knowing — they are, if anything, more striking than the paraphrase.
