# Acts 21: Paul in Jerusalem — Humility, Love, and Hostility

## Scripture Reference

> Read Acts 21:17-40 together before the study. Quoted phrases and key moments in this session:
> "God had done" the work among the Gentiles (v. 19) · the Jewish believers "glorified God" (v. 20) ·
> Paul was accused of defiling the temple (v. 28) · Lysias put Paul in "two chains" (v. 33) ·
> the crowd chanted, "Get rid of him!" (v. 36)
> — Acts 21:17-40

## Overview

Believers must be effective on *offense*, taking the gospel to the nations. But we also have to be effective on *defense*, "ready at any time to give a defense to anyone who asks . . . for a reason for the hope that is in [us]" (1 Pet 3:15). Usually the focus of fulfilling our Christian mission is on offense—initiating gospel conversations with unbelievers. But sometimes unbelievers may actually start gospel conversations with us, and in those situations we must be ready to explain why we're Christians.

For the last decade of his life Paul has intentionally engaged strategic centers with the gospel during his three missionary journeys. But now we'll observe Paul playing defense. The apostle becomes a prisoner, and the rest of his ministry recorded in Acts is basically a set of reactions to opposition and assaults. In Acts 21–26 we read of five defense speeches given by the apostle Paul and also learn what happens in between them.

**Main idea:** Paul gets attacked by a Jewish mob while participating in a vow, and then he gives a defense speech before the crowd.

In this session we consider Paul's welcome in Jerusalem (21:17-26) and the hostility of the Jewish crowd (21:27-36). In these we see an example of humility and love that we should pursue, and an example of hostility and lies that we may face.

## Key Points

### Point 1: A God-Centered Celebration (21:18-20a)

When Paul arrives in Jerusalem, things get interesting on day two. A formal meeting takes place between Paul, James (Jesus's half brother and primary spokesman of the Jerusalem church), and the Jerusalem elders.

- James and Paul together praise God for the victories achieved among the Gentiles. "God had done" the work among them (v. 19). Paul didn't brag; he gave glory to God.
- He also related "in detail" what God did on his journeys. It's a good practice to recount the evidences of God's grace and to share them with others. The psalmist says, "My soul, bless the LORD, and do not forget all his benefits" (103:2 ESV).
- When the Jewish brothers heard these things, they too "glorified God" (v. 20). The report concerned what God did among *the Gentiles*—and the Jewish Christians respond with joy, not jealousy or suspicion.

At times it's difficult for some Christians to rejoice when God is working through someone else or through someone else's church. But that's an attitude from which the follower of Jesus must repent. **We are never in competition with other like-minded Christians; we're on the same team!**

### Point 2: A Grace-Motivated Gift

At some point, in response to the grace of Jesus, certain Christians presented a love offering to the church in Jerusalem. This gift accomplished two purposes:

- It served as a means of helping the poor (cf. Acts 11:27-30; 20:35; 2 Cor 8:9; Gal 2:10; 6:10).
- It also served as a symbol of unity. In sending it, the Gentile Christians were humbly and lovingly expressing their solidarity with the Jewish Christians.

Unfortunately the gift in this case didn't actually accomplish the unifying purpose Paul hoped it would (Rom 15:31). Paul's reputation was suffering, so James gives Paul an interesting proposal in hopes of disarming the suspicions.

### Point 3: A Gospel-Driven Plan (21:20b-26)

The great news was that thousands of Jews trusted in Christ (v. 20b). But what hurt Paul's reputation was the word on the street that he was urging Jewish Christians living in Gentile communities to abandon their heritage.

James's concern was carefully limited:

- It wasn't about *salvation*—he and Paul agreed that salvation was by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone, apart from works of the law (see Acts 15; cf. 1 Cor 7:19; Gal 6:15).
- It wasn't about what Paul taught the *Gentiles*—the Jerusalem Council had already settled that (see 21:25; cf. 15:20,29).
- It wasn't about the *moral law*—both men agreed that regenerate people should pursue holiness.
- The concern had to do with *Jewish cultural practices* (Stott, *Message of Acts*, 341).

The rumors were untrue. Paul never demanded that the Jews abandon circumcision. In fact, his hurry to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost revealed that he still valued Israel's calendar (Johnson, *Let's Study Acts*, 266). Though Paul proclaimed that Christ was the end of the law (Rom 10:4), he didn't insist that Jewish believers give up Jewish customs (Rom 14–15; 1 Cor 8–10).

So James proposed that Paul demonstrate his respect for traditional Jewish Christianity by participating in a vow, going with four brothers who were soon to complete a Nazirite vow (cf. Num 6:1-21) and paying for the animals for their sacrifice. By fulfilling this request (v. 26), Paul showed that he didn't object to Jewish converts following Jewish customs as long as the gospel wasn't compromised and the customs weren't required of Gentiles.

Was this a compromise of the gospel? Paul's actions are in keeping with his missionary policy of becoming "all things to all people" for the sake of the gospel's spread (1 Cor 9:22-23). He lived out his words, "To the Jews I became like a Jew, to win Jews" (1 Cor 9:20). Paul could have said, "Let the haters hate. I'm going to do what I want!" But he didn't.

### Point 4: Hostility and Lies Displayed in the Jewish Mob (21:27-36)

From its focus on humility and love, Acts 21 turns to hostility and lies. What a stark contrast between the spirit of Paul and the spirit of the Jewish crowd.

- Some Asian Jews—probably from Ephesus (see 21:29)—recognize Paul and respond violently (vv. 27-28). Their wild accusations are designed to generate mob violence, and they sound like what Stephen's accusers said before stoning him (6:13-14).
- The accusations are ironic given that Paul was actually undergoing a purification ritual in order to enter the temple (in Stott, *Message of Acts*, 343).
- The Jews assumed Paul had brought Trophimus the Ephesian (v. 29; cf. 20:4) beyond the wall separating the court of the Gentiles from the inner sanctuary (cf. Eph 2:14). Of course, he had done no such thing.
- Lysias, the commander, arrests Paul and puts him in "two chains" (v. 33), perhaps indicating hand and foot, as Agabus predicted (v. 11). At the steps of the Antonia fortress the soldiers had to lift Paul up and carry him to protect him (v. 35).
- The crowd chanted, "Get rid of him!" (v. 36), just as an earlier crowd in Jerusalem demanded of Pontius Pilate, "Take this man away!" (Luke 23:18) and "Crucify him!" (Mark 15:13).

Did James's plan backfire? Paul didn't expect a pain-free experience in Jerusalem, for he knew afflictions awaited him there (cf. Acts 20:23; 21:4,10-11). His submission to James's plan was designed to show his support to Jewish Christians committed to their Jewish heritage, not to escape hardship. Paul's actions would eventually put the suspicions of many Jewish Christians to rest, and this event would be one link in the chain of sovereign events that would deliver Paul to Rome (Johnson, *Let's Study Acts*, 268).

### Point 5: Permission to Speak (21:37-40)

When Paul and the Roman soldiers reach the top of the stairs, Paul asks the tribune for permission to speak. The soldiers express surprise at Paul's proficient Greek. Lysias assumes Paul might be the Egyptian assassin who had recently stirred up a revolt (v. 38)—a false prophet who, according to Josephus, presented himself as a new Moses or Joshua and predicted Jerusalem's walls would fall like Jericho (in Polhill, *Acts*, 455).

Paul informs Lysias that he is neither a terrorist nor an Egyptian but a Jew and a citizen of the honorable city of Tarsus (v. 39), one of the great university cities in the Roman world (Stott, *Message of Acts*, 347). Upon learning of Paul's distinguished background, Lysias grants him permission to speak. Paul stands at the top of the steps, and the crowd falls silent as he addresses them in their own language (21:40; cf. 22:2; 26:14).

## Scripture Deep Dive

Why did Paul undergo a seven-day purification (v. 26)? John Polhill explains it was probably because Paul was returning from Gentile territory:

> Often a Jew on returning to the Holy Land after a sojourn in Gentile territory would undergo ritual purification. The period involved was seven days (cf. Num 19:12), which fits the present picture (v. 27). Paul thus underwent ritual purification to qualify for participation in the completion ceremony of the four Nazirites which took place within the sacred precincts of the temple. This would be a thorough demonstration of his full loyalty to the Torah, not only in his bearing the heavy expenses of the vow but also in his undergoing the necessary purification himself.
> — Polhill, *Acts*, 449

Since the minimum period for a Nazirite vow was thirty days, it seems Paul didn't undergo the Nazirite vow himself. He had, however, previously taken such a vow in 18:18 as a traditional Jewish expression of thanksgiving or petition—which in the words of I. Howard Marshall would be "theologically acceptable" and not a violation of the gospel (*Acts of the Apostles*, 300).

## Discussion Questions

1. What does it mean to defend the faith?
2. When might you need to defend the faith?
3. How do Paul and James display humility and love?
4. Compare Acts 21:20b-26 with 1 Corinthians 9:15-23. Are Paul's actions and his missionary policy consistent? Explain your answer.
5. Why were the Jewish people enraged at Paul?
6. In what modern contexts might Christians face hostility and false accusations? How should we respond to them?

## Application

We too should be flexible when ministering to various cultures. Some cultures are more traditional, while others are more progressive. When outside your immediate sphere, you may find it necessary to learn to adapt to the ways of another group for the sake of the gospel's spread. *Never compromise the gospel* and *never participate in sin* when you are attempting to reach people, but don't convey the impression that everyone must first be like you before they can take your invitation to accept Christ seriously. When the gospel is our main thing—when we find our identity in Christ rather than in an ethnic group, social class, or particular culture—then we will be able to minister humbly and lovingly with Pauline flexibility.

We must always make the right decisions in the sight of God and let things unfold as they may. Obedience to Jesus will involve hardship (2 Tim 3:12), so we shouldn't be surprised should intimidation, hate, and false accusations come our way. Early Christians were accused of incest, cannibalism, and atheism simply because they greeted one another with a holy kiss, took the Lord's Supper, and refused to worship the emperor. Today we're accused of immorality and bigotry because of our views on marriage and life.

When falsely accused and persecuted, remember that the Suffering Servant is with you. Jesus stands ready to grant you grace in time of need, and he will have the last word.

Also, look for ways to bless others through tangible gifts. Christians should regularly display acts of generosity because we have received such amazing grace from Jesus.
