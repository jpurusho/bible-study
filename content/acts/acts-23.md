# Acts 23: Jesus Stands with Us — Before the Council and Under God's Sovereign Hand

## Scripture Reference

> Read Acts 23 together before the study. Quoted phrases and key moments in this session:
> Ananias commanded those near Paul to "strike him on the mouth" (v. 2) · Paul called him a "whitewashed" wall (v. 3) ·
> "You must not speak evil of a ruler of your people" (v. 5; citing Exod 22:28) ·
> "the hope of the resurrection" (v. 6) · "Have courage! . . . You have testified about me in Jerusalem" (v. 11) ·
> the conspirators' oath not to eat or drink until they had murdered Paul (vv. 12-13) ·
> Lysias took the young man "by the hand" (v. 19) · two hundred infantrymen, seventy mounted soldiers, and two hundred "spearmen" (v. 23)
> — Acts 23

## Overview

**Main Idea:** Jesus was with Paul when he testified before the authorities, and Jesus is with us when we speak the gospel before unbelieving and unfriendly audiences.

It's one thing to know what to say about Jesus and how to say it, but there's also the challenge of finding the strength and encouragement to proclaim it. In this passage we see that the same Jesus who stood for Paul at the cross now stands by Paul in his trials (23:11). Jesus stood for us, and he now stands with us.

In this portion of Paul's afflictions in Jerusalem, the message of the book of 2 Corinthians comes to mind: "strength through weakness." We indeed see Paul afflicted, perplexed, persecuted, and struck down; but he's not crushed, driven to despair, abandoned, or destroyed. He knows Jesus is with him. **This section of Acts points us to the all-sufficient grace of Jesus, who uses wounded soldiers to accomplish his mission.**

It's not an easy task to understand or teach this passage because Paul's actions are simply reported here. Luke offers no appraisal of them. Bible teachers go in various directions when it comes to 23:1-10. One thing is for sure: Paul is a man with feet of clay. In this chapter we see his humanity and his need for the Lord's grace. It's a need we all share.

The second half of the chapter shifts the camera to God. The names of God, Jesus, and the Holy Spirit aren't mentioned in 23:12-35—a notable absence that recalls the book of Esther, where the name of God is missing but his fingerprints are everywhere within the story. **Never mistake the lack of the spectacular for the inactivity of God. His quiet, invisible hand is always at work.**

## Key Points

### Point 1: A Clear Conscience before the Council (23:1)

Paul "looked straight at" the Sanhedrin and offered his opening statement. First, he notes his own character—his actions are upright. The Sanhedrin included the high priestly aristocracy and the ruling elders, who were mainly Sadducees; the minority of members were Pharisees, represented among the scribes (Polhill, *Acts*, 470).

When it comes to defending the faith, our Christian character really matters (cf. 1 Tim 1:5; 1 Pet 3:14-16). We come to Jesus for a clean conscience (Heb 10:22). Paul told the Corinthians, "I am not conscious of anything against myself, but I am not justified by this. It is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor 4:4). In what he said, Paul didn't mean that he was sinless but that he lived above reproach and had done nothing to provoke the riot (see Acts 25:8). **We should pursue a blameless life, and when we're made aware of sin, we must humbly repent in order to maintain a good conscience and glorify God (see 24:16).**

### Point 2: "Rock" — Paul Rebukes the High Priest (23:2-5)

No sooner did Paul testify to the uprightness of his own character than the high priest, Ananias, commanded those near Paul to "strike him on the mouth" (23:2; cf. John 18:22). What a bizarre interruption! One would expect a leader in the Jewish high court to display civility and justice. Ananias's response was totally unacceptable and unbiblical (see Lev 19:15), and it makes his character stand out in stark contrast to Paul's.

Why such anger and aggression?

- First, Ananias thought Paul a wicked man—a troublemaker. But Paul wouldn't admit to any guilt, and this claim sounded absurd to Ananias, so he lost his temper.
- Second, Ananias *was* a wicked man. According to historical records, he was known for greed, a quick temper, violence, and pro-Roman sentiments.

Paul knew the law, and he knew that Ananias's order was unjust (Lev 19:15). He therefore threw a verbal rock back at him, calling out the hypocrisy of the high priest (cf. Deut 28:22; Ezek 13:10-16; Matt 23:27), who was likely dressed in his fine religious attire. Paul's language of a "whitewashed" wall was an insult: the priest outwardly looked clean and stable but was actually corrupt and flimsy. Paul's statement was more prophetic than he probably realized; within ten years Jewish freedom fighters would assassinate Ananias (Polhill, *Acts*, 468).

**How should we read Paul's words in verse 5?** Interpreters take two main views:

- The *sincerity* view — Paul genuinely didn't know he was speaking to the high priest. Maybe (1) Ananias wasn't dressed in religious garb since this wasn't a regular meeting of the Sanhedrin; (2) Paul didn't know who gave the order because of the commotion; (3) Paul's eyesight caused him to see only a blurry white-robed figure; or (4) Paul had been away from Jerusalem so long he didn't know Ananias.
- The *sarcasm* view — Paul was using irony, as if to say, "Brothers, I see nothing priestly in this man" (Lev 19:15; Deut 25:1; Calvin, "Christ the End of the Law," 318). If so, Paul is ministering in a prophetic sense: first the rebuke, then the strong statement about Ananias's character.

The commentary holds "(lightly!)" to the idea that Paul sincerely didn't know that the high priest gave the order. He sought to address people respectfully, and when he crossed the line, he admitted it. We too should pursue the proper tone with ruling authorities (Bock, *Acts*, 674–75), refusing to mimic the disrespect so rampant in our culture. And should we fail to speak as we ought, we should emulate Paul's humility.

While Paul spoke the truth, and while his cause was just, many correctly point out that his initial heated reaction doesn't reflect the spirit of passages like 1 Corinthians 4:12: "When we are reviled, we bless; when we are persecuted, we endure it" (cf. Matt 5:39). Jesus and Paul both condemned hypocrisy, but at the time of his trial our Lord responded to his accusers with restraint (see Luke 22:63-71; John 18:19-23). **While we must speak out against injustice, we must always do so with respect and restraint.**

### Point 3: "Scissors" — Paul Divides the Sadducees and Pharisees (23:6-10)

The trial takes a sharp turn when Paul, still in the middle of his speech, brings up the reason for his arrest: "the hope of the resurrection" (v. 6). His words end up "dividing and conquering."

Was Paul trying to divert attention away from himself? Perhaps. But Paul did indeed point out the real issue behind his trials: it was a theological dispute. His belief in Jesus's *resurrection* separated him from the rest of the Jews, and throughout his defense speeches he constantly brings up the resurrection (cf. 24:14-16; 26:6-8; 28:20). Further, at the end of this trial Jesus commended Paul for testifying about him in Jerusalem (23:11). So Paul was simply stating the facts.

- **Was Paul correct in calling himself a Pharisee and a son of Pharisees?** He was in fact educated in the pharisaic tradition (cf. 22:3; Phil 3:5). He wasn't necessarily claiming to endorse everything the Pharisees taught, but with respect to their eschatological hope revealed in Scripture (cf. Ezek 37:1-14; Dan 12:1-3), he still identified with them. **Jesus was the fulfillment of that hope.**
- The Sadducees didn't believe in the resurrection, nor in *angels*, nor the *spirit* (23:8). They only recognized the books of Moses as authoritative and argued that such ideas weren't found there. Jesus, however, showed them on one occasion that denying the resurrection contradicted God's covenant faithfulness to the patriarchs (Luke 20:27-39; Johnson, *Let's Study Acts*, 283).
- The effect of the controversy was sharp division. **The Pharisees actually defended Paul in this case!** As the story unfolds, others will reach the same conclusion about Paul's innocence (e.g., 23:29; 25:25-27; 26:32). But here the shouting soon turned to violence, and Lysias once again had to intervene and rescue Paul, delivering him back to the barracks.

The resurrection remains central to the Christian faith. Paul said, "If Christ has not been raised, then our proclamation is in vain, and so is your faith" (1 Cor 15:14). We must keep heralding the good news of the empty tomb, and we should expect various responses to it.

### Point 4: Reassurance — Four Encouragements (23:11)

Once Paul is back in the barracks, left to nurse his wounds and ponder what just happened, the Lord Jesus appears to the discouraged apostle. Paul was surely at a low point in his life: he had found an unsupportive church in Jerusalem, had suffered physically and emotionally before rulers and crowds, and may have questioned or regretted some of his actions. He was in need of the Lord's grace. **That's just what he received.** Jesus told Paul that he must go on to testify about him in Rome as he did in Jerusalem—a reminder that Paul's task wasn't mainly to defend himself; he was to testify about Jesus.

**1. The Lord knows us.** Jesus knew Paul's situation and condition, and he knows what we face, too. We are never outside his gaze. Jesus knows his sheep by name and by need (John 10:14).

**2. The Lord is with us.** Jesus's presence comforted Paul (cf. 18:9-10). While Paul felt alone, the reality is that Jesus was with him (cf. 2 Tim 4:17-18). Jesus stood for Paul at the cross; here he stands with Paul in the barracks. Be encouraged: the Lord will never leave you or forsake you (Heb 13:5).

**3. The Lord is for us.** The Lord displays his support in two ways. First, an exhortation: "Have courage!" (cf. Matt 9:2,22; 14:27; John 16:33)—the same exhortation he gave Paul in Corinth (Acts 18:9). Second, a commendation: "You have testified about me in Jerusalem." What an encouragement to hear the Lord say, "You did it. You told people about Me!" Charles Spurgeon said that Paul was "too humble to console himself with that fact till his Lord gave him leave to do so by acknowledging the brave deed!" ("Paul Cheered in Prison"). Paul asked the Romans, "If God is for us, who is against us?" (Rom 8:31).

**4. The Lord isn't finished with us.** Paul may have wondered if he would make it to Rome. But in telling Paul it was "necessary" that he testify there, the sovereign Lord let him know he would make sure he arrived. Though Paul would encounter many trials en route, he could face them knowing that the Lord's purposes would prevail. The psalmist said, "The course of my life is in your power" (Ps 31:15). **While we still have breath, we should believe that Jesus has work for us to do.**

### Point 5: God Uses an Unnamed Nephew to Thwart a Plot (23:12-22)

The day after Jesus reassured Paul in the barracks, more than forty angry Jews hatched a plot to kill him (vv. 12-13; cf. 9:24; 20:19). Their oath not to eat or drink until they had murdered the apostle meant they intended to act quickly. They approached the chief priests and elders—avoiding the minority Pharisees, who had defended Paul—and the Sanhedrin agreed to act as if they were reconvening to discuss Paul's crimes, so that the assassins could intercept and execute him on the way. So much for justice and law within the Sanhedrin (Gangel, *Acts*, 390)! These terrorists, like many modern-day terrorists, would stop at nothing to achieve their selfish, religious goals.

God, however, would thwart the scheme—and he used a young man to do it. John Polhill says it's anyone's guess how old the nephew was, but perhaps he was in his late teens (*Acts*, 472); in light of how Lysias takes him "by the hand" in verse 19, he may be just a little boy (Schreiner, "Living as Disciples"). Regardless, this unnamed nephew becomes an incredibly important advocate, one clearly raised up "for such a time as this" (Esth 4:14).

This is also the only mention of Paul's family found anywhere in the New Testament. We get the impression from his writings that he lost connection with them after his conversion (cf. Phil 3:8), but here we learn that he had a sister and a nephew.

**Marvel at the sovereignty of God here.** The Lord often uses little things and even little children to accomplish his great purposes. This story illustrates the seamless integration between God's sovereign agenda and human decisions made by responsible people (Johnson, *Let's Study Acts*, 288). The nephew hears of the conspiracy. He relates it to Paul. Paul acts wisely. The Roman centurion does his job. And Lysias acts to protect Paul. **There are no burning bushes involved in Paul's rescue. No light shows.** Paul's life is spared as a result of people doing what's in front of them.

### Point 6: God Uses Lysias and a Roman Army (23:23-35)

Lysias immediately responds to the threat. He makes plans to transfer Paul to Caesarea—named after Augustus Caesar, a beautiful harbor on the Mediterranean Sea that served as the headquarters for Roman military forces.

- To protect Paul, Lysias summoned the centurion to prepare two hundred infantrymen, seventy mounted soldiers, and two hundred "spearmen" (v. 23). **This action is a reminder that God can even use secular governments to achieve his purposes.** He turns the hearts of rulers and kings (cf. Prov 21:1).
- Lysias wrote a brief letter to the governor, Felix (vv. 26-30). He calls Felix "most excellent," tries to paint himself as a top-notch soldier, and conveniently omits that he nearly had a Roman citizen flogged illegally. But the most important statement in the letter is **the claim of Paul's innocence**: the problem involving him revolved around theology; it didn't warrant death or imprisonment. Luke once again weaves this theme into Acts: Christians aren't dangerous law-breakers (Schreiner, "Living as Disciples").
- Compare Corinth: after discouragement, the Lord reassures Paul of his presence and protection (18:9-10); angry Jews immediately attack (18:12-13); but Gallio dismisses their complaints as a quibble over theology (cp. 23:29 with 18:12-17).
- The soldiers take Paul to Antipatris by the cover of night, and the seventy horsemen then ride onward with him to Caesarea. **Though they assumed they were moving a prisoner, God was transporting his preacher safely.**
- Felix at least begins the right way, promising to hear Paul's case as soon as the accusers arrive, and sending him to be held by Herod's praetorian guard (v. 34).

So the nephew thwarts the plan. Lysias reports the plan. Soldiers transport the prisoner. And all of this occurs under the sovereign rule of the King.

## Scripture Deep Dive

On the Lord's intimate knowledge of his afflicted servants, Charles Spurgeon commented on this passage:

> One is reminded of the Quaker who came to see John Bunyan in prison and said to him, "Friend, the Lord sent me to you and I have been seeking you in half the prisons in England." "No, verily," said John, "that cannot be! For if the Lord had sent you to me, you would have come here at once, for He knows I have been here for years!"
> — Charles Spurgeon, "Paul Cheered in Prison"

Indeed, the Lord knew Paul's condition, and he knows ours.

On God's ordinary means of deliverance:

> Sometimes God delivers his children by the simple word of a young relative. Sometimes he has to call in the cavalry. At all times, he is ultimately in charge.
> — Kenneth Gangel, *Acts*, 391

And on where to look for the comfort Paul received, since we do not receive visions like his — the Lord still speaks to us by his Spirit, through the *Christ-centered Scriptures*:

> This is the whole of what we should seek in the Scriptures: to be well acquainted with Jesus Christ, and the infinite riches which are contained in Him; and which are, by Him, offered to us from God His Father.
> — John Calvin, "Christ the End"

The Pharisees had a category for resurrection but failed to rely on Jesus, who is himself the "resurrection and the life" (John 11:25). **Let your study of Scripture lead you to the hero of Scripture (John 5:39-40,46).**

## Discussion Questions

1. Read Luke 22:63-71 and John 18:19-23. Compare Jesus's reactions with Paul's reaction to Ananias. Do you think Paul responded appropriately? Why or why not?
2. Read Acts 24:14-16; 26:6-8; and 28:20. Notice how Paul emphasizes the resurrection. How do these passages relate to what Paul told the Sanhedrin in 23:6-10?
3. Why must we emphasize the resurrection when sharing the gospel?
4. How does verse 11 encourage you? Explain.
5. How can we experience the reassuring presence of Jesus on a daily basis?
6. Do you find it easier to affirm the sovereignty of God theoretically rather than resting in the sovereignty of God experientially? Explain your answer.
7. What does Acts 23:12-30 teach about the way God works to accomplish his purposes?
8. How do these assassins compare with modern-day terrorists?
9. What does Paul's life teach about living as law-abiding citizens?
10. Take a few moments to pray for God to grant you confidence in his sovereignty and courage in your witness.

## Application

**Speak with respect and restraint.** Paul's example in verses 2-5 cuts both ways: he was right to name injustice, and he was right to step back from the manner in which he named it. Pursue the proper tone with ruling authorities as you address them, refusing to mimic the disrespect so rampant in our culture. And should we fail to speak as we ought, we should emulate Paul's humility.

**Be an honorable citizen.** Christians shouldn't be ruthless pragmatists, breaking laws for the sake of our causes and claiming we are acting in the name of God. When the laws of the land don't hinder us from living out our faith, then we should abide by the rule of law as model citizens.

**This passage shows us that we can rest the whole weight of our concerns on God our Father, who holds the whole world in his hands.** Could it be that you find it easier to affirm the sovereignty of God *theoretically* rather than resting in the sovereignty of God *experientially*? God has an infinite number of options for working out his will in our lives. While our daily lives may not look spectacular, we can be assured that God is involved in the affairs of his people. Paul says, "I am sure of this, that he who started a good work in you will carry it on to completion until the day of Jesus Christ" (Phil 1:6). Trust him even in difficult circumstances! Thank him for his care and provision.

**Do what's in front of you.** God spared Paul's life through a nephew who spoke up, a centurion who did his job, and a commander who acted. The Lord often uses little things and even little children to accomplish his great purposes. Your ordinary faithfulness this week may be a link in a chain you cannot see.

**Don't neglect the Scriptures.** We won't receive a vision like Paul's in verse 11, but the Lord still speaks by his Spirit through his Word. By marinating our thoughts and hearts in the gospel, we find great strength.

**Memory aid.** The commentary labels Paul's three reactions "paper," "rock," and "scissors": paper = his Roman citizenship (22:22-29), rock = his rebuke of the high priest (22:30–23:5), scissors = dividing the council over the resurrection (23:6-10).

**Optional supporting passage for the overview.** 2 Corinthians 4:7-9 — "Now we have this treasure in clay jars, so that this extraordinary power may be from God and not from us. We are afflicted in every way but not crushed; we are perplexed but not in despair; we are persecuted but not abandoned; we are struck down but not destroyed" (cf. 2 Cor 6:3-10; 11:23-28; 12:9-12). Also I. Howard Marshall on the underlying conflict: "The real issue is not whether Paul defiled the temple, but whether Judaism was prepared to tolerate Christianity" (*Acts of the Apostles*, 360).

**Background on Felix (introduced in 23:24-35, developed in Acts 24).** A freed slave; a violent, licentious, and ineffective governor (procurator) of Judea (AD 52–59). He rose to office because of his brother Pallas, for a number of years the head of imperial civil service (Fernando, *Acts*, 578). He married three women of royal birth; Drusilla—daughter of Herod Agrippa I—was his third wife, a woman of legendary beauty (Thomas, *Acts*, 671) who apparently divorced her husband in order to marry him. He was eventually recalled to Rome for mishandling riots in Caesarea.

**Textual notes.** On page 357 the commentary prints a doubled "for": "one clearly raised up for 'for such a time as this' (Esth 4:14)." The quoted phrase from Esther is preserved above; the stray "for" is dropped.

On page 350 the commentary's sentence about Ananias's death reads, as printed, "In less than ten years, Ananias's Jewish freedom fighters would assassinate him (Polhill, *Acts*, 468)." This was verified against a high-resolution scan of the page. The point above renders the sense without reproducing the ambiguous possessive.
