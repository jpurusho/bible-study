# Acts 21–26: Paul's Five Defense Speeches — Broader Context

## Scripture Reference

> In Acts 21–26 we read of five defense speeches given by the apostle Paul and also learn what happens in between them. In the first two speeches Paul defends himself in Jerusalem before the Jewish crowd (22:1-21) and before the supreme Jewish council (23:1-6). His next three defenses are given in Caesarea. He goes before Felix in chapter 24, Festus in chapter 25, and finally King Herod Agrippa II in chapter 26. Because we, too, will face opposition, and because we must be good defenders of the faith like Paul, these chapters are relevant.
> — *Christ-Centered Exposition Commentary* on Acts, pages 334–335

## Overview

Believers must be effective on *offense*, taking the gospel to the nations. But we also have to be effective on *defense*, "ready at any time to give a defense to anyone who asks . . . for a reason for the hope that is in [us]" (1 Pet 3:15).

For the last decade of his life Paul has intentionally engaged strategic centers with the gospel during his three missionary journeys. But now we'll observe Paul playing defense. The apostle becomes a prisoner, and the rest of his ministry recorded in Acts is basically a set of reactions to opposition and assaults.

This session is a map of that whole stretch. Read it before or alongside the individual sessions on Acts 21, 22, and 23 so the group can see where each trial sits in the larger story — and so the repeated pattern becomes visible: **Paul is handed one hostile audience after another, and he turns each one into an occasion to talk about the resurrection of Jesus.**

**Main idea:** Across six chapters and five hearings, God moves Paul from a riot in Jerusalem to an appeal before Caesar — and in every setting Paul's task isn't mainly to defend himself but to testify about Jesus.

## Key Points

### The Setting: From Missionary to Prisoner (21:17-40)

Paul arrives in Jerusalem, is welcomed by James and the elders, and joins four men completing a Nazirite vow in order to demonstrate his respect for his Jewish heritage (21:17-26). Asian Jews recognize him in the temple, raise the false charge that he has defiled it, and a mob forms (21:27-36). Lysias the Roman commander arrests him and puts him in "two chains" (v. 33). At the top of the barracks stairs Paul asks permission to speak — and the crowd falls silent (21:37-40).

Everything that follows happens to a man in Roman custody. From here to the end of Acts, Paul is a prisoner.

### Speech 1: Before the Jewish Crowd in Jerusalem (22:1-21)

Paul's "defense" (*apologia*, 22:1) is a respectful and honest account of his life and conversion. He had two purposes: to show that he was **loyal** to his Jewish heritage, and to give the **honest** facts of his conversion — that his ministry wasn't the result of his own imagination and wild ideas but of the sovereign, transforming grace of Jesus.

- He recounts his former zeal as a strict Pharisee who persecuted Christians to the death (22:3-5).
- He recounts meeting the living Christ on the Damascus road (22:6-11) — Jesus *of Nazareth*, who told him that in persecuting the church he was persecuting the Lord himself.
- He recounts his commission through Ananias, a man with "a good reputation with all the Jews" (22:12-16).
- He recounts his vision in the temple (22:17-21) — an answer to the very charge that he had defiled it.

The speech ends the moment Paul says the word "Gentiles" (22:21-22). The mob erupts, and Paul never gets to address the temple charge. **Paul used this defense opportunity as an occasion to speak the good news.**

### Between the Speeches: "Paper" (22:22-29)

Lysias prepares to have Paul flogged. Paul asks one question — "Is it legal for you to scourge a man who is a Roman citizen and is uncondemned?" (v. 25) — and the examination stops. Paul was a citizen by birth; Lysias had bought his citizenship.

### Speech 2: Before the Sanhedrin (23:1-10)

Paul opens by appealing to his own upright character. The high priest Ananias orders him struck on the mouth; Paul answers with a prophetic rebuke, calling him a "whitewashed" wall, then steps back from the manner of his words when told he has spoken against a ruler of the people (23:2-5). He then names the real issue — "the hope of the resurrection" (v. 6) — and the council splits, because the Pharisees believed in resurrection and the Sadducees did not. **The Pharisees actually defended Paul in this case!** The shouting turns violent, and Lysias again rescues him.

That night the Lord Jesus stands by Paul and tells him he must testify in Rome as he has in Jerusalem (23:11) — a reminder that Paul's task wasn't mainly to defend himself; he was to testify about Jesus.

### Between the Speeches: The Plot and the Transfer (23:12-35)

More than forty men swear not to eat or drink until they have killed Paul. Paul's unnamed nephew hears of it and reports it; Lysias sends Paul to Caesarea under guard of two hundred infantrymen, seventy horsemen, and two hundred "spearmen," with a letter stating that the dispute is theological and warrants neither death nor imprisonment. **Never mistake the lack of the spectacular for the inactivity of God. His quiet, invisible hand is always at work.**

### Speech 3: Before Felix the Governor (24:1-27)

Chapter 24 gives a typical court procedure — the filing of charges, the prosecution of the plaintiff's spokesman, and the answer from the defendant (Johnson, *Let's Study Acts*, 293).

The prosecution hires a professional orator, Tertullus, who flatters Felix for a "great peace" his reign never produced, then brings four charges: Paul is a *pest* — a "plague" (v. 5); a *political agitator* who stirs up riots; *the leader of a sectarian movement* — the only place in the New Testament where the term "Nazarenes" describes Christians (Marshall, *Acts of the Apostles*, 375); and *disruptive in the temple* (v. 6). In a sentence, Tertullus charges, "Paul disturbs the peace as a seditious member of a dangerous sect" (Bock, *Acts*, 691).

Paul's **public witness** has three parts (Gangel, *Acts*, 411):

1. **A clear religious record (24:11-16).** He has been in Jerusalem only twelve days — it's difficult to start a riot in less than two weeks — and he takes pains to have a "clear conscience toward God and men" (v. 16).
2. **Blameless civil behavior (24:17-20).** He came bringing "charitable gifts" and presented "offerings" at the temple. His accusers from Asia are conspicuously absent from the trial; they had no case.
3. **A personal message: Jesus is alive (24:21).** Once again Paul funnels the hearing to the resurrection.

Then comes a **private context** (24:24-26). Felix postpones a decision and later sends for Paul, who speaks the same message in private that he spoke in public: "faith in Christ Jesus" (v. 24). He reasons about righteousness, self-control, and the coming judgment (v. 25) — three topics fitting Felix's lifestyle exactly. **The sermon, then, was not a nice, warm, uplifting talk. It involved confrontation with a call to repentance.** Felix was convicted by what he had heard but not changed. He kept summoning Paul, hoping for a bribe, and left him in prison two years.

### Speech 4: Before Festus, and the Appeal to Caesar (25:1-27)

Felix is replaced by Festus. The Jewish leaders press their charges again and ask for Paul to be transferred to Jerusalem, secretly intending to kill him on the way (25:1-5). At the judgment seat the accusers bring many serious charges they cannot prove, and Paul denies offending against the law, the temple, or Caesar (25:6-8).

When Festus — wanting to do the Jewish leaders a favor — proposes a trial in Jerusalem, Paul refuses and appeals to Caesar (25:9-12). It is the decisive turn of the whole section: the appeal is what will carry him to Rome, exactly as the Lord had said in 23:11.

King Agrippa II and Bernice then arrive on a state visit. Festus lays the case before Agrippa, admitting the dispute concerns the Jewish religion and one Jesus whom Paul claims is alive, and Agrippa asks to hear Paul himself (25:13-22). The hearing is convened with great ceremony, and Festus concedes publicly that Paul has done nothing deserving death — while admitting he has no charge to send to the emperor (25:23-27).

### Speech 5: Before King Agrippa II (26:1-32)

Paul's final and fullest defense. Given leave to speak, he addresses a king he describes as expert in Jewish customs and questions (26:1-3), and then traces his life:

- **His upbringing** — those who knew him from youth can testify he lived as a Pharisee, within the strictest sect (26:4-5).
- **The real charge** — he stands on trial over the hope of the promise God made to the fathers, the hope the twelve tribes pursue night and day (26:6-8).
- **His persecution of Christians** — he imprisoned believers by the chief priests' authority, cast his vote against them, and pursued them even to foreign cities (26:9-11).
- **The Damascus road** — at noon a light brighter than the sun, and a voice in Hebrew asking why he persecuted, identifying himself as Jesus (26:12-15).
- **His commission** — appointed a servant and witness, sent to Jews and Gentiles to open their eyes, turning them from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God, that they might receive forgiveness and an inheritance through faith (26:16-18).
- **His obedience and message** — not disobedient to the heavenly vision, he preached repentance and deeds to match, testifying nothing beyond what Moses and the prophets foretold: that the Christ would suffer, and that by his resurrection he would bring light to Israel and to the Gentiles (26:19-23).

Festus interrupts, calling him out of his mind; Paul answers that he is speaking words of truth and appeals to Agrippa, since none of this was done in a corner (26:24-27). Agrippa deflects with a question about becoming a Christian so quickly, and Paul answers that he wishes all his hearers were as he is — except for the chains (26:28-29). Withdrawing, the officials agree that Paul has done nothing deserving death or imprisonment, and Agrippa observes that he could have been released had he not appealed to Caesar (26:30-32).

## Scripture Deep Dive

Three threads run through all five hearings, and they are worth tracing with the group.

**First, the resurrection is always the point.** Paul's belief in Jesus's *resurrection* separated him from the rest of the Jews, and throughout his defense speeches he constantly brings up the resurrection (cf. 24:14-16; 26:6-8; 28:20). It is the hinge of speech 2 (23:6), the conclusion of speech 3 (24:21), and the climax of speech 5 (26:23).

> If Christ has not been raised, then our proclamation is in vain, and so is your faith.
> — 1 Corinthians 15:14

**Second, Paul is repeatedly declared innocent.** Lysias's letter says the matter doesn't warrant death or imprisonment (23:29); Festus says the same (25:25-27); Agrippa and his court say it again (26:32). Luke weaves this theme into Acts: Christians aren't dangerous law-breakers (Schreiner, "Living as Disciples").

**Third, God is steering the whole thing.** The Lord told Paul in the barracks that he must testify in Rome (23:11). A nephew overhears a plot; a commander mobilizes an army; a governor stalls for two years; a new governor offers a Jerusalem trial that forces an appeal to Caesar. **Marvel at the sovereignty of God here.**

> Sometimes God delivers his children by the simple word of a young relative. Sometimes he has to call in the cavalry. At all times, he is ultimately in charge.
> — Kenneth Gangel, *Acts*, 391

## Discussion Questions

1. Trace Paul's five audiences: a religious mob, a religious court, a corrupt governor, a new governor, and a king. What changes in how Paul speaks, and what stays the same?
2. Why do you think Paul brings up the resurrection in every single hearing?
3. In each of the five settings, Paul was the accused. How does he manage to keep turning the conversation to Jesus rather than to himself?
4. Three separate Roman authorities declare Paul innocent, and he stays in prison anyway. What does that teach us about the relationship between vindication and God's purposes?
5. Compare Felix (convicted but unchanged, 24:25) with Agrippa (deflecting with a question, 26:28). What are the ways people today keep the gospel at arm's length?
6. Paul told Agrippa he had not been "disobedient to the heavenly vision" (26:19). What has God made clear to you that obedience now requires?
7. Where in your own life are you being called to give an answer for the hope within you — and what would calm, courageous faithfulness look like there?
8. Take a few moments to pray for Christians who are on trial today for their witness.

## Application

**Be ready on defense, not just offense.** Usually the focus of fulfilling our Christian mission is on offense — initiating gospel conversations with unbelievers. But sometimes unbelievers may actually start gospel conversations with us, and in those situations we must be ready to explain why we're Christians.

**Look for the transition.** Paul first dealt with the charges and then gave a defense of the faith. He doesn't merely want to defend himself; he wants to proclaim the gospel as well. This is an excellent reminder to look for ways in your daily conversations to make gospel transitions as you talk with people.

**Expect opposition to be skillful.** Those who oppose Christ will go to great lengths to oppose the kingdom. They will use skill, political maneuvering, and lies. Such opposition shouldn't surprise us. We should be prepared spiritually for such attacks.

**Speak the whole message, including the hard parts.** Paul reasoned with Felix about righteousness, self-control, and the coming judgment. None of these points were popular then, nor are they today. But we must be more concerned about offending Christ than culture. Speak his Word in humility and love as a faithful messenger.

**Trust the sovereign hand you cannot see.** God has an infinite number of options for working out his will in our lives. While our daily lives may not look spectacular, we can be assured that God is involved in the affairs of his people. **While we still have breath, we should believe that Jesus has work for us to do.**
